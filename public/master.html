1. Your name and how to reach you

Recruiters shouldn't have to hunt for how to contact you. Name at the top, contact details right under it.

2. Your personal statement

This is the first thing they read about you. Be a person, not a press release. Answer a few of the questions below in your own words, then press Build my statement. You don't need a work history to write a good one: school, college, caring for someone, sport, volunteering and the things you love all count.

See what good looks like

Avoid: "A creative, structured professional with the ability to engage with customers in both effective and impactful ways."

Do this: "I'm a marketer who learned the trade running events for my mum's café. Twelve years on, I still believe the best campaigns start with knowing one customer properly. I work best in honest teams. My border collie thinks I should retire and walk her more."

From The Com'mon People CV guide. Specific, warm and memorable, and it sounds like a real person.

3. Key skills and behaviours

How you work: tick what sounds like you

Tick the ones that sound most like you. Each tick becomes a strength on your CV, written for you. The list includes traits many people with ADHD, autism, dyslexia and other kinds of neurodivergence recognise in themselves, written as the strengths they are. Nothing on your CV mentions a condition, and we never ask about one. Eight to twelve ticks works best on a CV.

Skills you can prove

Name the skill in a few words, then write a "prove it" line: what you actually did, and what came of it. "Rota planning: ran rotas for a team of 20 for three years" beats "excellent organiser". When you tailor your CV, the skills you lead with should match the job.

Stuck? Ideas for skills you can prove
  • Customer service: served 200 covers on a Saturday shift; regulars asked for me by name.
  • Cash handling: cashed up the till every closing shift for a year without a discrepancy.
  • Looking after people: cared for my grandad after his stroke, managing his medication and appointments.
  • Organising: planned our Year 11 prom for 120 students, including the budget and the DJ.
  • Leading a team: captain of the under-16s for two seasons; set up the training rota.
  • Digital skills: built and ran the Instagram page for my aunt's bakery, which grew to 1,400 followers.
  • Languages: fluent in Polish; translated for customers at my Saturday job.
  • Practical skills: rebuilt two bikes from parts and now fix friends' bikes.

Only write what's true for you. Small, real proof beats big, vague claims.

4. Work history

Latest first, then work backwards. For each one: what you did, then what you achieved. One line per point, and numbers help, like team sizes, targets and what changed because of you. Add your reason for leaving, because if you don't, the reader will make one up.

First CV, or no paid work yet? That's fine. Add Saturday jobs, work experience, volunteering, caring for family, running a club or team, a fundraiser, or a big project at school or college. Treat each one like a job: what you did, and what came of it. "Organised a bake sale that raised £240 for the hospice" is real experience.

Stuck? Ideas for what to write

Start each "what you did" line with a doing word: served, cooked, cleaned, cashed up, greeted, answered, restocked, delivered, looked after, trained, organised, planned, fixed, built, sold, set up, ran, sorted, checked, helped new starters.

For "what you achieved", think about:

  • Something you were praised, thanked or trusted with ("Trusted to open up on my own after three months").
  • Something that got better because of you ("Rearranged the stockroom so deliveries were put away in half the time").
  • Numbers: money raised, people served, hours worked, targets hit, days without missing a shift.
  • Anything new you learned or were given more responsibility for.
  • Awards, certificates or a good reference.

Reasons for leaving that read well: went back to college, moved house, a better opportunity, the job was seasonal, the contract ended, left to care for family, still here.

5. Interests

Underrated and powerful. Interests are how a stranger finds a reason to like you before you've met. Be specific ("trail running" beats "running"), be real, and mix a team thing, a solo thing and a creative thing. Volunteering counts. Three to six lines is plenty. One per line.

Tap an idea to add it, then make it yours: say which team, which games, which cause.

6. Education and training

Don't be vague: where, what, when and, if they're good, what you got. Add certificates and short courses too: food hygiene, first aid and licences all count. On a first CV, this is where you make your case, so use it.

Stuck? Ideas for what counts
  • GCSEs: list your subjects and grades, or say "9 GCSEs including English and Maths".
  • A levels, BTECs, T Levels, NVQs and diplomas, with grades if you have them.
  • Apprenticeships and traineeships, finished or in progress.
  • Food Hygiene Level 2, First Aid, Manual Handling, Fire Marshal.
  • Driving licence, CSCS card, Personal Licence, forklift licence.
  • Duke of Edinburgh, Prince's Trust, National Citizen Service, Scouts or Guides awards.
  • Online courses you finished, such as Google, OpenLearn or FutureLearn.
  • Still studying? Put "Expected" and the date.
